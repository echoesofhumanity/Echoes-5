SHORTER THAN THE OTHERS

A Story of a Lifetime
No one had told him how long he would live. Nor had he ever assumed his life would be long. He had simply grown accustomed to tomorrow coming. In the mornings, he opened the window, waited for the water to boil, sometimes placed a cup in the wrong spot on the table, and left the things he meant to do that evening until the following day. People believed in things that repeated themselves. In sunlight falling upon the same wall, in bread growing stale the next day, in spring returning after winter. He believed in them too. One day, he saw that a tree younger than himself cast a shadow older than he was. He knew the age of the tree. He did not know the age of its shadow. He stood there for a while. Then walked on. He did not yet know that a person could sometimes pass, without a second thought, a question whose answer he would spend his entire life searching for.
As a child, he had held the sea in his palm. When he closed his fingers, he imagined the whole sea belonged to him; when he opened them, he watched the water slip between his fingers and return to the world. His mother had laughed. He never learned how many seconds that laughter lasted. Yet years later, he could still remember the kitchen window, the soil around the basil plant, the scent of soap on his mother's hands. The house had been demolished. His mother had died. No one knew in which season the basil had withered. And still, on certain mornings, the window of that kitchen would open of its own accord in his memory. Human beings had learned to measure time. But they did not know what unit could measure the things they had lived.
At twenty-seven, they met at a railway station. The station clock had stopped. One was angry about a delayed train; the other was waiting for the rain to end. On the platform, a woman dropped her flowers. A child let go of his mother's hand. A man ran and caught an arriving train. The clock was still stopped. No one cared that it had stopped. Trains came and went, people met and parted, entered one another's lives, and sometimes never crossed paths again. “Sometimes a minute lasts longer than a lifetime,” one of them said. The other laughed. “Then what shall we measure a lifetime with?” They did not answer. For a while, they watched the rain. Then they began walking together. By the end of that day, neither knew how long they would remain in the other's life. But when a person first drew close to someone, he did not ask for the date of their parting.
Then he fell in love. He learned that the woman he loved sometimes did not wish to speak in the mornings, and on certain evenings could tell the same story all over again. They laughed together. They grew angry with each other. One day, over something insignificant, they spent an entire afternoon without speaking. That evening, the woman handed him a glass of water. He took it. The silence between them wished to stay a little longer. They both let it. Sometimes loving someone meant being beside them without having to say anything at all. And sometimes it meant not postponing what needed to be said. But they did not always manage that. There were times they hurt each other. What one forgot, the other remembered for years. And yet their love had not grown from believing each other perfect. They had agreed to discover each other's imperfections anew, every day.
They had set aside a book for the following summer. Inside it were people they had not yet met, cities they had not yet visited. They would read it by the sea. The woman said she would not get up early in the mornings. He agreed to prepare breakfast. “What if I don't like the book?” she asked. “We'll find another.” It was that simple. Another book could replace a book, another morning could follow a morning, another summer could come after a summer. Or so they believed. Because a person could make plans for the future without ever considering whether that future truly belonged to them. When someone fell in love, the future grew more crowded. Two cups, two towels, two books left open on the same table... Days that had not yet been lived were already finding places for themselves inside the house.
One day, by the sea, they found a small piece of blue glass. The waves had carried it for years, wearing away its edges. It could no longer cut anyone's hand. “Do people become like this too?” the woman asked. “I don't know,” he said. “Some of us grow sharper each time we break.” The woman held the glass up to the light. “And some of us hide that we have been broken.” They were silent for a while. They both knew that not every wound taught a person something. Some pain was simply pain. Some losses made nothing more beautiful. Still, the woman slipped the blue glass into her pocket. She liked seeing that something once broken could still let the light through.
He loved being alive. The man selling roasted chestnuts on the corner, the stones darkening after rain, a child able to ask the same question seven times. Sometimes he was angry with the world. With injustice, with the human talent for hurting one another, with the ease with which adults could overlook a child's fear. He knew the world's imperfections. And despite them, he wanted to wake for another morning, sit beneath a tree, hear the voice of the woman he loved. Once, he said: “I get so angry with this world. But I still want to be here.” The woman smiled. “So do I.” Then they went shopping. They bought tomatoes for dinner. Decided one was not ripe enough and put it back on the stall. Life went on like this, with small ordinary tasks between great thoughts.
Then, one morning, tomorrow did not come for him. The sun rose. The baker opened his shop. Children hurried to school. A woman missed her bus. The sea touched the stones along its shore with the same patience as the day before. The world continued. For a long time, the woman he loved was angry with it. How could it? How could a person's entire future come to an end within a single day, while the futures of others went on as though nothing had happened? How could it be that on the same morning, every clock in one house lost its meaning, while in another, a child counted the days until his birthday? After a while, she began to understand that the world's continuing did not make him insignificant. But the thought did nothing to ease her pain. Some truths did not exist to comfort us.
The book remained on the shelf. At first, the woman could not bring herself to touch it. Then one day she opened it. She read the first page. Turned to the second. She did not like the book. When she realized this, she cried. Because once, they had said they could always choose another. Now she could choose another book. But not with him. It was not the book that had been left unfinished. It was a time they had imagined together, a time they had not yet lived. The woman closed the book. The following morning, she went to work. Years later, she loved someone else. At first, she found it difficult to explain that love to herself. It took time to learn that growing accustomed to someone's absence was not the same as forgetting them. Her new love did not take the place of the one before. Because people were not beings who could be put in one another's place. She lived in another house. Woke to different mornings. Laughed again. One day, she realized she could no longer remember the exact sound of her former lover's voice. She was silent for a long time. Then she heard the person beside her saying something. She turned her head. She listened. That day, she understood a little better that life did not ask the living to apologize for going on.
The other person he had met at the station went on living. His years multiplied. He traveled to other cities. Loved other people. Parted from some. Lost others. His hair turned white. Lines appeared on his face that had never been there before. The names of familiar streets changed. Some houses were demolished. Some trees were cut down. One day, he found an old photograph. The three of them stood together by the sea. The man whose life had been short, the woman he had loved, and himself. They were laughing in the photograph. He could not remember why. And yet, for years, he had spoken of how beautiful that day had been. For a moment, he thought that remembering was also a kind of forgetting. Memory was not a perfect room in which the past was kept. With every recollection, it rearranged some of the furniture. He noticed that the sea in the photograph was calmer than the sea in his memory. He had always remembered that day as rainy. But the sky was clear. Where had he brought the rain from? Perhaps from the station. Perhaps from another farewell. Perhaps from an afternoon that had never happened. He did not know. For the first time, he saw so clearly that when we remembered those we loved, we could not preserve them exactly as they had been. And still, he did not stop remembering.
At seventy-eight, he found a small piece of blue glass at the bottom of a drawer. The woman had given it to him years before, as they returned from that day by the sea. “So it won't get lost,” she had said. It had not been lost. But how much had been lost since that day. He held the glass in his palm. He thought of his friend, who as a child had held the sea in his hand. That person was no longer alive. Yet having once existed in the same world as him was still part of his own life. He walked to the window. Across the street stood a tree. It was younger than he was. Its shadow stretched all the way to the other end of the street. He remembered a question he had heard many years before: “Then what shall we measure a lifetime with?” This time, too, he could not answer. He knew how many years a person had lived. But he did not know how much space a laugh, a touch, an ordinary afternoon spent together could occupy inside another human being. There was no way to measure it. And now, he no longer wished to.
That evening, he went outside. He bought a warm loaf of bread from a bakery he had passed for years. It began to rain. Once, he would have sought shelter. This time, he walked slowly. He saw a child jump with both feet into a puddle on the pavement. The child's mother scolded him. The child laughed. He laughed too. He did not wonder which laughter in his life this laughter mattered more than. Nor how many seconds it lasted. He simply laughed.
When night came, he left the window open. From far away came the sound of a train. He thought of the station where two strangers had once waited beneath a clock that had stopped. One of them had been given less time than the others. The other had remained in the world long enough to live the years his friend had never lived. But no year had ever taken the place of a year someone else had been denied. No new love had proved the one before it false. No happiness had repaid the debt of an earlier happiness. No loss had been undone by wisdom gained afterward. And still, life, after all of this, remained something that could be loved again. Outside, people were talking. A door closed. The wind turned the leaves of the tree. Somewhere, people no one had yet met were being born. Somewhere, someone was making plans for next summer. Somewhere, two people were falling in love. Perhaps one of them did not know it yet. Perhaps the other was waiting for the right moment to say so. The old man smiled. The clock passed midnight. He did not notice.
The following morning, he placed a small glass of water on the windowsill. He did not know why. Perhaps for the birds. Perhaps to watch how the sun moved within the water. Perhaps there was no reason at all. As the day grew brighter, the light in the glass fell upon the wall. A small, trembling sea appeared there. The man pulled up a chair and sat down. He watched it for a long time. Once, a child had held the sea in his palm. Once, two people had set aside a book for next summer. Once, a clock had stopped at a railway station, but life had not. All these things had happened. None of them still existed in quite the same way. The man reached out his hand. The light fell into his palm. He did not close his fingers. For a while, he remained like that. Then, from outside, came the voice of a child. The man turned his head. And the light quietly slipped away from his palm.
